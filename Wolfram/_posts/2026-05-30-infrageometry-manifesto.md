# Infrageometry Manifesto

The purpose of this manifesto is to distinguish our effort in Infrageometry from ordinary discrete geometry. We are not primarily asking how to discretize aspects of a smooth manifold and encode as much geometry as possible combinatorially, or how to associate a geometry to a graph based on its embedding, manifold learning, and coarse equivalence. Even though understanding this pipeline, implementing it, and building bridges to it, for example through bounds on geometries that make the associated geometry less fuzzy, are also part of the work, that is not our central question.

Instead, we are asking which notions of geometry are native to the discrete substrate and which notions would be developed by a discrete, computationally bounded observer within it. To do that, we intentionally set aside the existing mathematics that arose historically from macro-observers modeling the macroworld through abstract, idealized, and utterly uncomputable notions, and consider only what is needed and natural at the discrete observer's scale on sufficiently large graphs. We attempt to explain geometric aspects of the universe, such as dimension, gauge degrees of freedom, observer independence, and relativity, as emergent and persistent aggregated properties of particular (hyper)graphs along certain rewriting paths. We also want to pursue this computationally, with code present at every step.

To do that, we start by defining and implementing the least complex natural geometric notions and ask whether they can branch out and reproduce structures known from continuous models in the limit of many rewriting steps. We then test this empirically by setting up computational experiments and developing methods for detecting emergent patterns and laws between aggregated observables. We also plan to formulate and prove limit theorems about the resulting limiting structures.

We think about how the seemingly continuous, unbounded, and unique macroworld, might emerge from a discrete one characterized by indivisibility, boundedness, and ambiguity. Given advances of physics in uncovering homogeneous microstructure by probing ever smaller scales, this is arguably the more natural picture from the point of view of fundamental theories of nature. We do not attempt to model macroscopic effects efficiently by starting from idealized notions. It is enough for us to build a coherent framework and demonstrate emergence. Similar ideas could also apply to the origins of life, chemistry, and physics.

See my [talks in Srní and Madrid][talks] for some very early work, see also the [Austin conference post][austin-post] (to be published), and stay tuned for more mature work coming out later in 2026.
